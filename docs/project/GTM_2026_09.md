## 0. The decision this document forces

There are three different companies described in this repository, and they are mutually exclusive.

| Source | The company it describes | Status |
|---|---|---|
| `docs/marketing/` and `docs/project/` (Oct 2025 to Dec 2025) | Social entertainment platform. 30+ party games, AR treasure hunts, AI highlight reels | The games were deleted. No media router exists. Dead. |
| `docs/mobile-ios-launch/01-product-scope.md` (approved 2026-09-05) | Free host utility. One host, 50 guests, private events, browser RSVP, no payment surface | Approved and being built |
| `server/routes/billing.ts` and `server/lib/entitlement.ts` (committed 2026-09-14) | AI planning copilot with a metered free tier and two trials | Shipped this week, undocumented anywhere else |

The code committed most recently is the strongest signal of intent, and it is unambiguous. `server/lib/entitlement.ts:27-33` sets `FREE_AI_TURNS = 3`, `TEASER_TRIAL_DAYS = 7`, `CARD_TRIAL_DAYS = 14`. `prisma/schema.prisma:1519` constrains `plan` to `free | pro`. `prisma/schema.prisma:1530` constrains `source` to `none | stripe | apple`. The metered unit is the LLM turn, counted at `server/routes/billing.ts:58` and deliberately excluding the deterministic fallback.

Nothing else in the product is metered. Not guests, not events, not storage, not invitations.

**The strategic position this document adopts:**

> PartyHause sells an AI planning copilot. The event tool is free forever and exists to acquire, retain, and give the copilot something to act on.

Three reasons this is the correct read rather than an opportunistic one.

**One. You cannot charge for a guest list in 2026.** Partiful is free. Luma is free. Facebook Events is free. Google Calendar is free. The RSVP-tracking category was commoditized to zero by venture money and there is no path back. Every prior pricing table in this repository gates on guest count, which means every prior pricing table proposed charging for the one thing three funded competitors give away.

**Two. The onboarding schema is a product bet already placed.** `prisma/schema.prisma:1659-1684` records `ai_before`, `ai_during`, `ai_after`, `autonomy_level` and `agentic_wants` against every new account. `packages/core/src/contracts/billing.ts:91-100` defines the autonomy ladder as `suggest | draft | act_with_approval | act_autonomously`. Somebody already decided the research question is "how much work will you let an agent do for you," and built a table to answer it. That is not a guest-list company.

**Three. The price anchor is different and better.** A guest-list tool is anchored against other apps, where the ceiling is zero. A planning copilot is anchored against the event budget, where a $12 monthly fee is a rounding error on a $1,800 party.

### The iOS conflict, and how it resolves

`docs/mobile-ios-launch/01-product-scope.md` makes `IOS-PAY-01` mandatory: the iOS app is free and contains no purchase, subscription, or payment link surface. That is directly incompatible with shipping a paid tier inside the iOS app.

Do not reopen the approved scope. Resolve it structurally instead:

- **The iOS app ships exactly as approved.** Free, private events, 50 guests, no payment surface. It passes review on the first attempt, which is worth more than early iOS revenue.
- **The paid tier lives on web.** A subscriber who signs up at `partyhause.com` and signs into the iOS app is entitled, because `GET /api/billing/entitlement` is server-computed and platform-agnostic by design (`server/lib/entitlement.ts:11-18` makes this explicit). The iOS app reads `can_use_ai` and shows the feature. It never sells it.
- **This is the standard reader-app shape.** Spotify, Kindle and Netflix all do it. It also means web-acquired subscribers carry no platform commission.
- Revisit Apple in-app purchase only when web conversion is proven and the 15 to 30 percent is worth paying for incremental volume. `source: 'apple'` already exists in the schema for that day (`prisma/schema.prisma:1530`).

## 2. The funnel is already coded. Market against it.

This is the most useful thing in the repository and no marketing document mentions it. `computeEntitlement` in `server/lib/entitlement.ts:158-203` defines a precise five-state machine, and `paywallReason` at `:212-230` names each exit. Marketing does not need to invent a funnel. It needs to fill one that already exists.

**Corrected 2026-09-14.** An earlier draft of this section described a four-stage funnel with a 7-day no-card teaser trial. That trial was designed and cut. `server/lib/entitlement.ts:194` now offers `can_start: { card_14d }` only. `docs/ROADMAP.md` section 4 records the reasoning: the onboarding paywall is hard, and a free path beside it makes it soft. The funnel is three stages, not four.

```
SIGNUP
  ↓  onboarding screens 1-3
3 FREE LLM TURNS, lifetime          FREE_AI_TURNS = 3
  ↓  paywall.reason = 'free_quota_exhausted'
HARD PAYWALL, screen 4              no dismiss; only entitlement clears it
  ↓
CARD TRIAL, 14 days, card on file   CARD_TRIAL_DAYS = 14
  ↓  paywall.reason = 'subscription_required'
PAID
  ↓  paywall.reason = 'subscription_lapsed'
WIN-BACK
```

The wall sits at screen 4 of 5, after the user has answered who they are and where they want the model to help, and before the autonomy question. `docs/ROADMAP.md` section 5 has the full design. Three consequences for marketing copy:

**The wall must only quote capabilities that exist.** A paywall that says "you want RSVP chasing and recap drafting, that is Pro" describes software that does not exist, and the first refund request would be correct. Quote the planning capabilities, which are real.

**The three free turns are the entire free product.** There is no trial beside them. Every acquisition asset has to carry a user to a genuine "that was useful" inside three turns, which raises the bar on the demo in section 3.1 considerably.

**Dunning copy is recovery, not warning.** `server/lib/entitlement.ts:170` keeps `past_due` users entitled until the period they already paid for elapses, deliberately, so a declined renewal is not an immediate lockout.

### Target conversion rates, and the volume they imply

These are targets, not measurements. Nothing has been measured because the paywall is not live.

| Step | Target | If it misses, the diagnosis is |
|---|---|---|
| Signup to first AI turn | 60% | Onboarding buries the planner. Screen 1 is the chat for exactly this reason. |
| Activated to 3 turns exhausted | 30% | **Product problem, not funnel problem.** Someone who used the planner once and stopped did not find it useful. Fix the planner before fixing the ads. |
| Exhausted to card entered | 12% | The hard wall's real number, and the one everything depends on. Three turns did not produce enough value to justify a card. |
| Card trial to paid | 50% | Card trials convert high because the card is on file. Below 35% means the trial length is wrong, not the price. |

Compounded: **signup to paid is roughly 1.1%.** That is a normal consumer freemium number for a hard wall and it should not be argued with. It means:

- 100 paying subscribers needs about **9,300 signups**
- 1,000 paying subscribers needs about **93,000 signups**

The hard wall trades volume for signal. It converts worse than a no-card trial would and it produces a cleaner read on whether the product is worth money, because everyone past it has paid rather than merely consented. That is the right trade at this stage, but it raises the top-of-funnel requirement by roughly 50% against the cut design, and section 3 has to carry that.

Any plan that does not produce signups at that order of magnitude does not produce revenue. This is why section 3 rejects the tactics in the old documents.

### The viral loop, rewritten 2026-09-14 for the attendee-profile decision

**This section previously argued the loop was a CAC reducer at roughly k = 0.6 and could not grow the business. That analysis assumed guests never create accounts. That assumption is now wrong.**

The confirmed direction is that a guest registers before they can RSVP, and the account they create is an attendee profile. It shipped as `20260915000000_rsvp_requires_account`, enforced in `server/lib/rsvp.ts` by `resolveBinding`, with invitations issued before the change grandfathered behind a `requires_account` flag.

That changes the arithmetic in both directions and the net is strongly positive.

**What gets better.** Every RSVP is now a registration. A host with 20 accepted guests produces up to 20 accounts, not zero. The invitation stops being an impression and becomes an acquisition event. This is the single largest change to the growth model available, and it is now built rather than proposed.

**What gets worse.** A signup wall sits between an invited person and the answer their host is waiting for. Expect RSVP completion to fall. The relevant comparison is not "20 accounts versus 0" but "20 accounts and a lower reply rate versus 0 accounts and a higher one", and the host feels the second number immediately.

**The numbers to hold.** These are targets, not measurements, and the first two are the ones to instrument first.

| Metric | Target | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Invitation open to RSVP page | 70% | Unchanged by this decision. `resolve` stays public. |
| RSVP page to registration started | 55% | The new wall. The number that decides whether this was right. |
| Registration started to RSVP recorded | 80% | Signup with a valid token auto-verifies, so there is no inbox round trip. |
| Net invitation to RSVP recorded | **~31%** | Against roughly 55% with no wall. A material drop, bought deliberately. |

At 20 invitations that is about 6 registered attendees per event where there were none. Even at the pessimistic end this dominates a 3% guest-to-host conversion, because **the account now exists before anyone decides to host anything.** The attendee is acquired first and converted to host later, which is a fundamentally better ordering than hoping a guest remembers the brand months afterwards.

**The loop is now a genuine growth engine rather than a CAC reducer.** That is the reversal, and it is earned by a product change, not by re-forecasting.

### The three things that decide whether this works

**One. The event must be visible before the wall.** `RsvpService.resolve` is deliberately unauthenticated (`server/lib/rsvp.ts`), so an invitee sees what they are being invited to before being asked to register. This is the difference between a wall and a door. If anyone ever puts `requireAuth` on `resolve` as a tidying exercise, the loop dies that day.

**Two. Registration must not cost an inbox round trip.** Signup bearing a valid invitation token auto-verifies against the invited address. Without that, a guest goes invitation, signup, inbox, confirm, back to RSVP, which is four context switches on the one flow where a host is waiting for a number.

**Three. The attendee profile has to be worth having.** A guest who registers and receives nothing has been taxed for the host's convenience. The profile must carry something back: the events they are attending, what they said yes to, when and where, and what they owe. `UserProfile` and the `@handle` layer already exist. This is the part with no owner on the roadmap and it is the part that makes the trade honest.

### What this does not change

Host-to-host referral is still weak, and attendee-to-host conversion is still the hard step. An attendee account is a warm lead, not a host. The content and search work in section 3 still has to carry top of funnel for **hosts**, which is a different acquisition problem from acquiring attendees and should not be conflated with it now that the two have separated.

## 3. Getting free users for the trial phase

### The one product change that matters more than every tactic below

`server/routes/ai.ts:44` gates `POST /api/ai/chat` behind `requireAuth`. **That is a growth defect.** The entire value of this product is in the first AI turn, and it is currently behind a signup wall and an email verification wall.

Gate at the save, not at the ask.

- Let an anonymous visitor run **one** planning turn with no account. Rate limit by IP, cap the response, and store nothing.
- When they see the plan, require an account to keep it. They now have something to lose, which is what converts.
- The remaining two free turns stay behind auth, so `FREE_AI_TURNS = 3` is unchanged and `billableAiTurns` accounting stays honest.

Expected effect on landing-to-signup is a multiple, not a percentage. Every content asset in this section becomes twice as effective because it can end in a working demo instead of a signup form.

Implementation note: this needs a distinct anonymous path, not a weakening of `requireAuth`, and it must not write `ai_usage` rows for anonymous turns or the quota count at `server/routes/billing.ts:58` becomes meaningless.

### Phase A. Hand-to-hand, 0 to 200 hosts, weeks 1 to 6

**Do not launch on Product Hunt first.** Product Hunt delivers developers. Developers do not throw parties that need a guest list, they do not have a mother turning 60, and they will not convert. The old documents put Product Hunt on day 3 of a 7-day plan, which optimises for a vanity spike and a 2% D7 retention curve. Save it for when there is a real product story and real testimonials, around month 4.

Go where people are already failing at this in public.

**A1. The planner in the replies.** The highest-leverage free tactic available, and it is the only one that uses the actual product as the ad.

People post their planning problems in specific, findable places. Someone writes "I'm doing a 40th for 35 people at my house and I have no idea where to start." You paste their exact description into the AI planner, screenshot the output, and reply with it. No link in the first reply. The plan is the reply.

This works for reasons most content does not. The artefact is genuinely useful to that specific person. It is not an ad, so it survives moderation in groups that ban promotion. It demonstrates the product without a click. And it is repeatable at roughly two minutes per reply.

Target 10 per day for six weeks. That is 420 replies, each seen by the thread, at zero cost.

Where to find them:
- r/weddingplanning, r/EventPlanning, r/Parenting, r/bachelorette, r/travel, r/Cooking (dinner parties), r/sorority
- Facebook groups for specific event types. These are large, active, and the members are non-technical, which is the point.
- Quora and TikTok comments on planning-panic videos

**A2. White-glove for the first 50 hosts.** Offer to build the event for them, personally. Take their description on a call or over DM, set up the event, write the invitations, and hand them the login.

This is deliberately unscalable, which is why it works. It produces the three things the business cannot get any other way: a real case study, footage for UGC, and a user who will actually answer a question about why they did or did not pay.

Airbnb photographed listings by hand. Stripe integrated for customers by hand. Do this for 50 hosts and then stop.

**A3. Seed the recurring hosts first, not the once-a-year hosts.** The onboarding schema measures `events_per_year` at `1-2 | 3-10 | 11-50 | 50+` (`packages/core/src/contracts/billing.ts:103`). The `11-50` and `50+` cohorts are a small minority of signups and almost all of the subscription value, because their need is continuous rather than episodic. A subscription product that acquires only `1-2` hosts churns by construction.

Find them specifically: supper club organisers, run club leaders, church and community group coordinators, university society social secretaries, house-show promoters, board game meetup hosts. They plan monthly. They are underserved by everything. And they are reachable because they already run a public page.

### Phase B. Loop mechanics, 200 to 2,000 hosts

Four surfaces already exist and none of them currently converts anyone. Build these before spending on ads.

**B1. Post-RSVP conversion.** When a guest hits accept on the RSVP page, that is the highest-intent moment in the entire product. They have just experienced the product working, on the receiving end, at an emotional moment. Today they see a confirmation and leave.

Add one line: "Planning something yourself? Start free." It should carry the host's event context, because "you just RSVP'd to a 30th, here is what planning one looks like" outperforms a generic prompt.

This single surface is the whole difference between k = 0.6 and k = 0.3.

**B2. The invitation email footer.** Every invitation is a branded impression delivered to a non-user at a moment of anticipation. `server/lib/invitation-renderer.ts` renders a fixed template. Add a footer that links to a create flow, not a homepage. At 20 invites per event and 1,000 events, that is 20,000 addressed impressions a month for zero marginal cost.

Keep it small and keep it honest. A loud footer on someone's wedding invitation is a reason to not use the product.

**B3. Co-host invites.** Inviting a co-host forces a real signup from someone who is already collaborating on an event. `EventCoHost` exists in the schema and the web app supports it. This is the highest-quality signup the product can generate, because the new account arrives with an event already in it. Note this is excluded from the iOS MVP, so it is a web-only loop for now.

**B4. Handles as a public surface.** The username layer shipped 2026-09-13 (`server/lib/username.ts`). `partyhause.com/@handle` is a link a recurring host can put in an Instagram bio. For the supper club and meetup organisers in A3, that is their actual distribution. Make the public profile good enough to be worth linking to.

### Phase C. Category entry, 2,000 hosts and up

**C1. Search, built on the 11 templates.** The product ships 11 event templates. Each one is a content cluster with real, high-intent, low-competition long-tail search behind it.

Do not write "best event planning software." That term is owned and the traffic does not convert for a consumer product. Write the thing the host actually types at 11pm:

- "30th birthday party timeline for 40 people"
- "bachelorette weekend itinerary template"
- "kids birthday party schedule 3 year olds"
- "how much food for a block party of 60"
- "hackathon run of show template"

Every page ends in the anonymous planner from section 3.1, pre-filled with that page's scenario. The article is the ad and the demo is the call to action. This is the only channel here with compounding returns, and it takes four months to start, so start it in week one.

**C2. Paid, only after the funnel is measured.** Do not spend on ads until `GET /api/billing/entitlement` has produced real conversion data across all five states. Spending to acquire users into an unmeasured funnel is how the $300,000 phase-one budget in the old GTM document would have been burned.

When it is measured, the buy is Meta and TikTok against life-event and interest signals (engaged, new parent, recently moved), creative sourced from section 5, and the success metric is cost per card trial started, not cost per signup.

**C3. Product Hunt, month 4 or later.** With testimonials, a working paywall, and the anonymous demo live.

### What to explicitly not do

| Tactic from prior documents | Why not |
|---|---|
| Festival booths, guerrilla QR stickers, bar coasters (`JOIN_THE_BUBBLE_STRATEGY.md`) | The entire play depends on QR crew-scanning. No camera permission, no QR, PartyCrew excluded from MVP. There is no product behind the $50,000. |
| "Lifetime Pro access for the first 100 testers" | Giving away the only revenue line in perpetuity, before a price exists, to the cohort most likely to convert. |
| Campus ambassador programmes | High overhead, students plan free events on Instagram, and nobody in that segment pays for software. |
| Wedding bridal shows | Weddings are the highest pain and the worst retention. Once per customer, then churn. Sell the adjacent events instead. See section 4. |
| Influencer seeding at 50 to 100 micro-influencers | Expensive, and creator content outperforms sponsored content for utility products. See section 5. |

## 4. What events people actually create, and what to say to each

The 11 shipped templates are a hypothesis. Ranked by realistic volume, coordination pain, budget, and whether the host recurs.

| Template | Volume | Pain | Budget | Recurs | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Birthday Party (Adult) | Very high | Medium | $300 to $3,000 | Yearly | **Beachhead** |
| Group Travel | Medium | **Extreme** | $500 to $8,000 | 1 to 2 per year | **Beachhead** |
| Kids Birthday Party | Very high | High | $150 to $800 | Yearly, per child | **Beachhead** |
| Wedding | Low | Extreme | $10,000+ | Once | Sell the satellites, not the wedding |
| Block Party, Fundraiser | Medium | Medium | Low | Annual | Community segment. Good retention, low ARPU |
| Class/Workshop | Medium | Low | Varies | **Weekly or monthly** | **Best subscription fit in the list** |
| Conference, Product Launch, Hackathon | Low | High | High | Varies | Corporate. Different motion. Not now. |
| Music Festival | Very low | Extreme | Very high | Annual | Wrong product. Ignore. |

### Beachhead 1: the milestone birthday

The 30th, 40th, 50th, 60th. Not an ordinary birthday, which needs no software, but the one where you invite 30 to 80 people and book something.

Why it is the best single wedge: it has real volume, real coordination pain, a real budget, and a cohort effect nobody in the prior documents noticed. **Everyone in a friend group turns 30 within about two years of each other.** One host running a good 30th means 19 friends watched the tool work, and several of them have the same problem coming within 18 months. That is the closest thing this product has to organic compounding, and it is why the post-RSVP prompt in B1 matters so much.

The message: it is not about organisation. The host's actual fear is the number. They will book and cater for 40 and 22 will show up, or they will plan for 25 and 40 will arrive. Both are expensive and both are humiliating.

> "You booked for 40. Twenty two turned up. You paid for both."
>
> PartyHause shows you who opened the invite, who clicked, and who actually replied. Then it tells you what to order.

The delivery telemetry in `email_logs` and `email_events` is the only feature in this product that reduces genuine anxiety. Lead with it.

### Beachhead 2: group travel and the bachelorette weekend

The highest-pain event that exists. Six to fifteen people, four hundred unread group chat messages, nobody has booked anything, one person is fronting every payment, and the social cost of asking for money three times is the worst part of the entire experience.

Two things make this the most valuable segment.

First, the whole group becomes users, not just the host. A bachelorette weekend is twelve people all inside the same coordination problem for six weeks.

Second, this is where `cost-split` matters. `server/routes/cost-split.ts` has complete CRUD and no interface. **Building that interface is the highest-return product work available.** Asking friends for money is a social problem, and turning it into a software problem is worth paying for in a way that storing a guest list never will be.

The message:

> "You fronted £2,400. You have asked three times. Two people have still not paid."
>
> PartyHause tracks who owes what and asks for you.

**This message cannot be used until the cost-split UI ships.** Flagged again in section 5.

### Beachhead 3: the kids birthday

Highest raw frequency in the list. One child, one party, every year, for roughly twelve years, and most households have more than one child.

The pain is specific and it is not the same as the adult version: you are coordinating with other parents who do not reply, who reply for the wrong child, who bring an extra sibling, and who need to know about allergies and pickup times. The parent network is also the densest referral graph available. One school class is 25 households who all have the same problem on a rolling basis.

The message:

> "Twelve kids invited. Four parents replied. One of them replied for the wrong child."
>
> Send it once. PartyHause chases the rest.

### The wedding correction

Prior documents targeted weddings directly. That is a retention trap. A wedding is one event, once, and the customer is gone the day after.

The satellites are the business. One engagement produces an engagement party, a bridal shower, a bachelorette weekend, a bachelor weekend, a rehearsal dinner and often a post-wedding brunch. That is five to six events across eighteen months, hosted by **different people**, most of whom are not the couple. The maid of honour who plans the bachelorette is a first-class customer with her own network.

Target the satellites. Let the wedding come along for free.

### The corporate segment

`organizer_type: professional | corporate` is already measured at onboarding (`packages/core/src/contracts/billing.ts:102`). These users have the highest willingness to pay and the most continuous need.

They are also a different sales motion, a different security posture, and a different support burden. Do not chase them in the first two quarters. Do watch the onboarding data, because if `professional` and `corporate` are converting at multiples of `personal`, that is the signal to pivot the whole company and it will show up in the `OnboardingResponse` table before it shows up anywhere else.

## 5. Brand voice

### The correction to the existing position

`docs/BRAND.md:34-42` sets the brand idea as "Raise the roof," describes the tone as "warm, energetic, competent," and retains the tagline `Plan. Party. Perfect.` from `src/components/AuthScreen.tsx:364`.

The brand idea is good. The mark is good. The tagline is weak and should be replaced.

`Plan. Party. Perfect.` fails on three counts. It is a tricolon of abstractions, which is the single most common shape in software marketing and therefore invisible. Two of the three words are not verbs the user performs. And "Perfect" is a promise the product cannot keep against a promise the customer is not making. The host is not trying to be perfect. The host is trying to not be anxious. Selling perfection to an anxious person raises the stakes they are already struggling with.

The App Store subtitle already approved in `docs/mobile-ios-launch/01-product-scope.md`, **"Plan, invite, welcome,"** is better: three concrete verbs in the order they happen, and the third one is the emotional payoff.

Recommended primary line, for the AI-copilot position:

> **Tell it what you're planning.**

It is the actual first interaction, it implies the AI without saying "AI," it is a complete sentence, and it is impossible to confuse with a guest-list tool. Keep "Raise the roof" as the brand idea behind the mark, where it belongs.

### The governing principle

**The confetti lives in the logo. It does not live in the copy.**

The customer's emotional state before they use this product is low-grade dread. They are hosting, which means they are responsible, which means the ways it can go wrong are all their fault. A brand that shouts at an anxious person increases the anxiety. A brand that is calm and specific reduces it.

So the visual identity carries the party, which is what the coral and the confetti and the lifted roof already do well. The language carries the competence. If both shout, the product reads as chaos, which is precisely what the customer is paying to escape.

`docs/BRAND.md:40` already got this right: "like a good night out that someone actually organised, not like chaos." The copy has not followed.

### Four voice pillars

**1. Specific, never general.** Numbers, not adjectives. "Forty people, two hours of food, one person doing the maths" beats "streamline your event planning." Specificity is the whole voice. If a sentence would work for a project management tool, delete it.

**2. Calm under pressure.** The product's job is to be the person in the room who has done this before. That voice does not use exclamation marks, does not use urgency, and does not congratulate you for completing a form. It tells you what is true and what happens next.

**3. Funny about the problem, never about the product.** The humour points at the shared experience of hosting, which everyone recognises: the group chat, the person who replies "maybe" and means no, the sibling who brings four extra people. It never points at the software, because a product joking about itself is a product asking to be liked.

**4. Honest to a fault.** This repository already has a culture of it. `README.md:50` opens a status section with "because a README that oversells is worse than one that undersells." `src/pages/VendorDashboard.tsx` says the marketplace is unbuilt rather than showing zeros. That is rare and it is worth more than any slogan. Make it the brand. Say what the product does not do.

### Lexicon

| Use | Never use |
|---|---|
| host, guests, plan, invite, reply, the night | users, attendees, stakeholders, engagement |
| "who is actually coming" | "RSVP management" |
| "what it costs, and who owes what" | "financial reconciliation" |
| "the group chat" | "communication silos" |
| "tell it what you're planning" | "leverage AI to optimise" |
| "it drafts, you send" | "AI-powered automation suite" |
| plain numbers: 40 people, 7 days, £2,400 | "seamlessly", "effortlessly", "unforgettable", "elevate", "unleash", "revolutionise", "game-changing" |

Hard rules. No em dashes. No emoji in interface chrome, navigation, or as a brand mark, which `docs/BRAND.md:278` already mandates. No exclamation marks in product copy. No "🎉" anywhere near the brand.

## 6. UGC scripts

### What actually works, and what does not

Be blunt about this, because most UGC plans produce polished videos that get 400 views.

**Works for a utility product in 2026:**
- Cold open on the pain with no product for the first three seconds
- Screen recording with a voiceover of a real, specific problem
- The receipt: someone shows a result and says where it came from
- Stitch or duet replying to someone else's planning panic
- Anti-ad framing: "nobody paid me, this is just what I used"

- Founder to camera saying "hey guys, I built an app"
- Feature tours and UI walkthroughs
- Launch countdowns
- Text-on-screen listicles of features
- Anything that looks like it had a budget

**The most reliable UGC engine here is not a paid creator. ## 7. Recurring revenue

### The structural problem, stated plainly

**Events are episodic. Subscriptions are continuous.**

A typical host plans three events a year. They will not pay monthly for twelve months to use something three times, and if they do, they will notice in month five and cancel. This is why Evite, Punchbowl and Paperless Post all monetize per send or per event, and why Partiful and Luma are free and monetize elsewhere entirely.

Every prior pricing table in this repository ignores this. Any subscription plan has to answer one question: **why am I paying in March when my party is in July?**

There are only four honest answers, and the product has evidence for three of them.

1. **The copilot is useful in the gap.** "Should I do this at all," "what will this cost," "who should I invite." Real but weak on its own.
2. **Recurring hosts exist and are measured.** `events_per_year: 11-50 | 50+`. Supper clubs, meetups, community organisers, workshop leaders. A minority of signups and the majority of subscription value.
3. **Professionals exist and are measured.** `organizer_type: professional | corporate`. Continuous need by definition.
4. **The account accrues.** Past events, guest lists, who came, who did not eat what. That is retention rather than revenue, but it is what makes annual plans renew.

The conclusion is not "do not do subscriptions." It is: **a subscription-only model will leave most of the revenue on the table, because most hosts are episodic.** Both shapes are needed.

### The seven revenue lines, ranked

Ranked by durability times build cost times regulatory risk.

---

**1. Pro subscription. Scaffolding shipped, no price, no processor.**

*State:* `plan: free | pro` (`prisma/schema.prisma:1519`), full entitlement state machine, two trials, four paywall reasons. No Stripe. No price anywhere in the codebase.

*Recommendation:* **One tier. Two prices.** Do not build four tiers. Four tiers is a decision the customer has to make, and every prior document in this repository proposed a different four.

Recommended entry point, to be price-tested, not assumed:

| | Price | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly | £12 / $12 | Below the $20 consumer AI anchor, above the $8 "cheap utility" band that signals a toy |
| Annual | £84 / $84 | 42% off. Smooths the seasonality of an episodic product and converts the March problem into a single decision |

Anchor the copy against the event budget, never against other apps. A 40th costs £800 to £3,000. At £84 a year for three events that is £28 an event, or under 2% of the party. Say that.

*Build required:* Stripe Checkout and the customer portal, `POST /api/billing/trial`, webhook handling into `BillingEvent`, and gating `POST /api/ai/chat` on `can_use_ai`. `server/routes/billing.ts:3-7` says explicitly that none of this exists yet.

---

**2. Event Pass, one-time. NOT BUILT. This is the largest missing revenue line.**

*The gap:* the funnel has exactly one exit, and it is a subscription. The wedding host, the once-a-year milestone host and the bachelorette organiser are the highest-intent, highest-budget users in the product, and every one of them will refuse a subscription for a thing they do once. Today they hit `subscription_required` and leave.

*Recommendation:* a one-time **£19 to £29 Event Pass** that unlocks the full copilot, the higher guest cap and cost-split, for one event, for 90 days.

Why this matters more than it looks:
- It converts the majority who will never subscribe. For an episodic product this is most of the revenue, not a minor supplement.
- It is more honest than a subscription for a once-a-year customer, which matters given the brand position in section 5.
- It is the natural upsell ladder. Buy a second pass in a year and the annual plan sells itself with arithmetic: "you have spent £48 on two events. The year is £84 and covers everything."
- It works on iOS later as a StoreKit consumable, which is simpler than a subscription.

*Build required:* a `plan` value or a separate entitlement grant scoped to one `event_id`, and a checkout. `computeEntitlement` would need an event-scoped branch, which is a real change to a function that is currently cleanly global. Design it deliberately.

---

**3. Cost-split. API complete, no UI, no payments.**

*State:* `server/routes/cost-split.ts` has full CRUD. `CostSplitRequest` and `EventCostSummary` models exist. No money moves.

Two versions, and the distinction is important.

**Version A, ship this.** Calculate the split, track who has paid, send the reminders, and hand off to Monzo, Revolut, Venmo or PayPal.me deep links. **No money touches PartyHause.** No payment licence, no KYC, no regulatory exposure. It is a paid feature of Pro and the Event Pass, and it is the single strongest conversion argument the product has for group travel.

**Version B, do not ship this yet.** Actually processing the money for a take rate. This is a payments business. It means money transmission licensing, KYC and AML obligations, chargeback liability, and on iOS a direct collision with `IOS-PAY-01`. The revenue is real. The regulatory weight is a company-changing commitment, not a feature. Revisit in 2027 with counsel, and only if Version A shows real volume.

*Priority:* Version A is the **highest-return unbuilt work in the product.** It converts the highest-pain segment, it unblocks two UGC scripts, and the API is already written.

---

**4. Professional and corporate tier. Measured, not built.**

`organizer_type: professional | corporate` is captured at onboarding today. A wedding planner or a corporate events coordinator has continuous need, which solves the March problem outright, and a much higher willingness to pay.

The features they need mostly exist as data already: multi-event dashboards, delivery and attendance analytics (`GET /api/email-logs/analytics/event` is live), guest list export, co-hosts and team access, and custom branding on invitations.

*Recommendation:* do not build this tier on speculation. Watch the `OnboardingResponse` table. If `professional` and `corporate` convert at a multiple of `personal`, that is the signal, and it will appear in that table months before it appears in revenue. A £39 to £79 monthly tier is defensible in that segment.

---

**5. Premium invitation designs. Models exist, no design assets.**

`InviteTemplate` and `/api/invite-templates` are live. Evite and Paperless Post have monetized premium invitation designs profitably for twenty years. It is a proven, high-margin, one-time purchase.

Honest counterweight: it is a declining category, it is design-labour-intensive rather than engineering-intensive, and it does not fit the copilot position. Worth doing if a designer is cheap, worth skipping otherwise. It does pair well with the venue decorator in section 8, since both are aesthetic products.

---

**6. Vendor marketplace. Models only, no routes, and correctly labelled unbuilt.**

`Vendor` and `VendorTask` exist in the schema. `src/pages/VendorDashboard.tsx` honestly says the marketplace is not built.

The classic events monetization is vendor commission on caterers, DJs, photographers and venues, and the money is genuinely large. It is also a two-sided marketplace, which means a cold-start problem on both sides, geographic density requirements, and a sales team. It is not a near-term recurring revenue line for a product with a few thousand hosts.

It is, however, the natural destination for the venue decorator. See section 8.

---

**7. Affiliate on physical goods. Not built.**

Every event has a shopping list. Decorations, catering supplies, rentals, party goods. Affiliate commission on that list is low-margin per transaction and requires no licensing, no marketplace and no sales team.

Alone it is not a business. Attached to a decoration plan that names specific items with specific quantities, it is a real line. This is the revenue mechanism for section 8.

### The honest summary

**Exactly one recurring revenue line is coded today, it has no price, and it cannot take a payment.** Everything else is a model without a route, a route without an interface, or a plan.

The revenue-critical build order:

1. Stripe plus `POST /api/billing/trial` plus gate `/api/ai/chat` on `can_use_ai`. Without this there is no revenue at all.
2. Cost-split interface, Version A. Highest conversion return per engineering day.
3. Event Pass. Captures the episodic majority the subscription cannot.
4. Anonymous first AI turn (section 3.1). Not revenue, but it multiplies the top of the funnel that everything above depends on.

## 8. Sidecar: the agentic venue decorator

Future development. Not in the current build order. Specified here so the decision is informed rather than improvised later.

### What it is

Give it a venue, an event type, a budget and a theme. It returns a decoration plan: a zoned layout, a shopping list with real purchasable items and current prices, a setup sequence with timings and people required, and a visual render of the result.

Not a mood board. A plan you can execute.

### Why it fits this product specifically

**The context already exists.** The `Event` record carries theme, budget, location, guest count and date. A generic decoration tool has to ask for all of it. This one already knows.

**It is the biggest unsolved part of hosting.** Search volume for decoration ideas is enormous and the category is owned by Pinterest, which supplies infinite inspiration and zero executable plans. The gap between "this looks lovely" and "I have a room, £200, and four hours" is the entire product.

**It is the bridge to the vendor marketplace.** A decoration plan with real SKUs is affiliate revenue on day one (line 7). A plan the host does not want to execute themselves is a qualified vendor lead (line 6). It solves the marketplace cold-start by generating demand before supply is needed.

**The autonomy ladder is already in the database.** `packages/core/src/contracts/billing.ts:91-100` defines `suggest | draft | act_with_approval | act_autonomously`, and `agentic_wants` is an array field collecting exactly this research. The decorator maps onto that ladder cleanly, which is unusual and suggests somebody was already thinking about it:

| Autonomy level | What the decorator does |
|---|---|
| `suggest` | Here are three directions for your room and budget |
| `draft` | Here is the full plan, the list and the layout. You buy it. |
| `act_with_approval` | Here is a filled cart at £187. Approve and I place it. |
| `act_autonomously` | Booked. A decorator arrives at 14:00 on the 12th. Here is the receipt. |

**It is shareable, which makes it a growth loop.** A render of your actual living room decorated for your actual party is a thing people post. Nothing else in this product is.

### Architecture

Five passes. Each one is independently valuable, which matters because it means this ships incrementally rather than as one large bet.

```
venue input ──► VISION ──► PLAN ──► SOURCE ──► RENDER ──► EXECUTE
 photo/desc      geometry   zones    real SKUs   image      cart or
 + Event ctx     features   items    live price  preview    vendor brief
```

**1. Vision.** Venue photographs via `POST /api/storage/upload`, which exists (multer, 5MB, jpeg/png/webp/gif allowlist). Extract room geometry, ceiling height, fixed features, light sources, focal wall, existing colour palette, and usable floor area. Degrade gracefully to a text description when no photo is supplied, because many hosts are decorating a venue they have not visited.

**2. Plan.** Produce **structured data, not prose.** Zones with placements, items with quantities, a setup sequence with durations and people required. Prose is unverifiable, unpriceable and unrenderable. A schema is all three.

**3. Source.** Resolve every planned item to a real, purchasable SKU with a current price. **This is the hard part and it is the moat.** Retailer affiliate feeds or a curated catalog. Everything else in this feature is an API call somebody else can make.

**4. Render.** Image generation conditioned on the original venue photograph.

**5. Execute.** Cart handoff, or a structured brief to a vendor if the host wants it installed.

### Invariants, non-negotiable

Applying Power of 10 rules 2 and 5 to an agent that spends money.

| Invariant | Why |
|---|---|
| The plan total never exceeds the stated budget. Asserted after sourcing, before display. | An agent that overspends its brief is worse than no agent. This is the feature's entire credibility. |
| Planning iterations bounded at 3. Return the best plan found on exhaustion, never loop. | Rule 2. Unbounded agent loops burn tokens and wall clock with no termination proof. |
| Prices and availability are re-checked at cart time, never trusted from plan time. | Affiliate feeds go stale within hours. A plan that prices at £180 and carts at £260 destroys trust permanently. |
| No money moves without explicit human approval, at every autonomy level including `act_autonomously`. | `act_autonomously` means "do it and tell me afterwards" for actions, not for spend. Approve the budget envelope once, not each item, but approve it. |
| Every sourced item carries a provenance record: retailer, price, timestamp, affiliate status. | Disclosure is a legal requirement, and an undisclosed affiliate link in a paid product is a brand-ending story. |

### Honest risks

**Spatial consistency in generated renders is still unreliable.** As of 2026, conditioning image generation on a specific real room and getting a result that is both attractive and geometrically faithful is not solved. If the render is presented as a preview and the reality does not match, the product has manufactured disappointment on the customer's most emotionally loaded day. Present it as direction, label it, and never use the word "preview."

**Affiliate feeds are a maintenance burden, not a one-time integration.** Prices move, items delist, regional availability differs. This is an ongoing operational cost that the feature's margin has to cover.

**It is a photo-permission feature.** `docs/mobile-ios-launch/02-information-architecture.md` declares zero sensitive-permission prompts as a release invariant. So this is web-first, or a deliberately scoped post-MVP iOS release with a new permission and a new review.

**It has genuine competition.** Interior design AI tools already exist and some do rooms well. The defensible difference is event context and executability: they redesign a room permanently for a homeowner, this one dresses a room temporarily for a specific event on a specific date with a specific guest count. Do not let it drift into general interior design, where it loses.

### Build sequence

**v0, no vision model, no image generation.** Template-based decoration plans by event type and budget tier, sourced from a curated catalog of two to three hundred items. Host picks event type, budget and theme, and gets a real list with real prices and a text layout.

This is the correct first version and it is cheap. **If a hand-curated plan with a real shopping list does not convert, the AI version will not convert either**, it will just cost ten times more to discover that. Ship v0, measure whether anyone carts, and only then decide.

**v1.** Add the vision pass and the LLM planner. Photograph in, bespoke plan out, still sourced from the curated catalog.

**v2.** Add the render, and broaden sourcing to live affiliate feeds.

**v3.** Add `act_with_approval`: the filled cart and one-tap purchase.

**v4.** Add the vendor brief, which is also the vendor marketplace's first real demand signal.

### Revenue

The only unbuilt feature in this product with three revenue lines and a growth loop at once.

| Line | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Subscription | A Pro and Event Pass feature. A strong reason to pay that has nothing to do with guest lists. |
| Affiliate | Commission on the shopping list. Requires no licensing, no marketplace, no sales team. |
| Vendor lead | A host who wants it installed is a qualified, priced, dated lead. This is how the marketplace starts. |
| Growth | The render is the only genuinely shareable artefact the product can produce. |

## 9. Live play: games, reinstated

Reinstated by decision on 2026-09-14, reversing the position taken in section 0 of the first draft of this document. The reversal is justified, but only under a different frame, and the frame is the whole point.

### What was wrong before, and what is different now

The first draft killed games. That was correct about the **claims** and wrong about the **capability**, and those are not the same judgement.

What was genuinely wrong with the 2025 position: it claimed 30 games existed when zero did, it made an unbuilt entertainment library the primary moat, and it priced tiers against games that could not be demonstrated. All of that stays dead. The number 30 is never used again, and no game is marketed before it ships.

What the first draft under-weighted, and what changes the answer:

**Every other surface in this product is host-facing.** Section 2 established that the invitation loop runs at roughly k = 0.6 and therefore decays. The reason is that a guest's entire experience of PartyHause is one email and one RSVP page. Live play is the only surface where all fifty guests actively use the product, on their own phones, at the emotional peak of the event. That is the single largest change available to the coefficient, and nothing else in the roadmap touches it.

**It is the content the during-phase agent was built to run.** The agent harness is a live-event engine with nothing live to run beyond timeline drift and attendance. Games give it something to do, and the agent gives games something no competitor can copy. See below.

**The competitive space is genuinely empty.** Luma, Partiful, Evite and Punchbowl all stop at the door. Not one of them has any product that operates during the event. That is unusual and it is not an accident of neglect: they are invitation companies. Occupying the hours the invitation was for is a different category and it is unclaimed.

### The reframe

> Games are not an entertainment library. They are the guest acquisition surface, and bespoke game content is what the AI subscription buys.

This matters because it decides what gets built. An entertainment library optimises for catalogue size, which is how you end up claiming 30 games. A guest acquisition surface optimises for **join rate and completion rate**, which means few games, each excellent, each launched by the host at a chosen moment.

### Why it strengthens the paid tier rather than competing with it

The strongest version of this is not "we have trivia." Kahoot has trivia. Jackbox has better trivia.

The version nobody can copy is trivia **about the guest of honour**, generated from what this product already knows: the event, the host, the guest list, the occasion, and a handful of answers the host gives the copilot. Kahoot cannot do that because Kahoot does not know whose birthday it is.

That produces a clean monetization line that does not tax the guest:

| Tier | Live play |
|---|---|
| Free | The engine, and two games with generic content |
| Pro / Event Pass | Bespoke AI-generated content for every game, host-authored custom rounds, the full catalogue |
| **Guests** | **Always free, never an account, never a paywall** |

Charging a guest, or making a guest sign up to play, destroys the acquisition surface to collect nothing. This is a hard rule.

### Positioning language

Permitted, once shipped and only for what has shipped:

- "The invitation gets them there. The rest of it is the party."
- "Trivia about the birthday girl, written from what you told us."
- "No app. They scan, they play."

Prohibited, permanently:

- Any count of games, especially "30+"
- "Entertainment ecosystem", "Play Together", "the party is the platform"
- Any game shown in an asset before it is live for every user

### Sequencing

Live play is **not** in the next 30 days and does not compete with the revenue work in section 10. The order is: payments, then cost-split, then the agent's during-phase, then live play on top of it. Games without a payment processor is a feature with no business attached, which is precisely the mistake of the 2025 plan.

The engineering plan, including the guest-identity blocker that prevented this from ever working before, is in `docs/LIVE_PLAY.md`.
